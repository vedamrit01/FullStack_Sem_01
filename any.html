Sheet 1 · foundation

What communication really is

Resources: Communication Intro (skillsyouneed) · Communication Styles (asana)

1.Definition

Communication = transfer of meaning AND understanding between people. If the receiver didn't understand, it didn't happen. It is two-way — feedback closes the loop.

2.The process

Sender → encode → message → channel → decode → receiver → feedback ↺

Noise can enter at any step: a loud room, jargon, a bad mood, a weak signal.

e.g. Manager emails "ASAP" (encode) → intern reads it at 11 pm (decode: panic) → replies "done?" (feedback). Same word, different meaning.

3.Types

  • Verbal — spoken words (meetings, calls, presentations).
  • Written — emails, reports, chats. Permanent → be careful.
  • Nonverbal — face, posture, gestures, eye contact, tone.
  • Visual — charts, slides, diagrams.
  • Listening — half of all communication; hearing ≠ listening.

4.The 7 Cs (checklist for any message)

CAsk yourself
ClearOne purpose? Easy words?
ConciseCan I cut words without losing meaning?
ConcreteFacts, numbers, dates instead of vague claims
CorrectGrammar, spelling, facts, names
CoherentPoints connect logically
CompleteReader knows what to do next
CourteousPolite, respectful, reader-focused

5.Barriers

  • Physical — noise, distance, bad network.
  • Language / semantic — jargon, ambiguous words, accent.
  • Psychological — stress, anger, low confidence.
  • Cultural — gestures and eye contact mean different things.
  • Perceptual — assumptions, stereotypes, not listening.
  • Information overload — too much, too fast.

Fix: know the audience, simple words, check understanding, choose the right channel.

6.Four communication styles

StyleSounds likeResult
Passive"Whatever works for you."Own needs ignored; resentment builds
Aggressive"Just do it my way."Others feel attacked; trust drops
Passive-aggressive"Fine. Sure." (sarcasm, sulking)Says yes, acts no; confusing
Assertive"I need the draft by Friday — can you manage?"Honest + respectful; best outcome
Aim = assertive

Use "I" statements: "I feel ___ when ___ because ___. Could we ___?" Firm on the issue, soft on the person.

7.Active listening

  • Full attention — phone away, eye contact, nod.
  • Paraphrase: "So what you're saying is…"
  • Ask open questions; don't interrupt; don't plan your reply while they talk.
  • Summarise at the end to confirm.

One line: Communication is meaning understood, not words sent — encode for your audience, kill the noise, be assertive, and listen to understand.

Sheet 2 · RC

Reading comprehension

Exam part 1 · Reading Comprehension

1.The 4-step method

  1. Skim (1 min): title, first + last paragraph, first line of each paragraph → topic + author's view.
  2. Glance at the questions so you know what to hunt for.
  3. Read actively: underline the main claim, circle tone words, box signal words.
  4. Answer from the passage, not from what you know. Go back and find the line.

2.Question types

TypeHow to crack it
Main idea / titleCovers the whole passage, not one paragraph
Specific detailLocate the line; match meaning, not just words
InferenceMust be true based on the text; one small step, no leap
Vocabulary in contextReplace the word with each option; reread the sentence
Tone / attitudeLook at adjectives and adverbs the author chooses
Author's purposeWhy written? to inform / argue / criticise / compare

3.Trap answers

!! eliminate these first
  • Too extreme: always, never, all, only, completely.
  • Out of scope: sounds sensible, but the passage never says it.
  • Distortion: uses the passage's words with a twisted meaning.
  • True but irrelevant: correct fact, wrong question.
  • Half right: first half fits, second half doesn't.

4.Signal words

SignalWordsMeaning
Contrasthowever, but, yet, although, despitethe author's real point usually comes after
Cause / resulttherefore, hence, thus, so, as a resultconclusion follows
Additionmoreover, furthermore, alsosame direction, more support
Examplefor instance, such as, e.g.evidence, not the main idea

5.Tone vocabulary

  • Positive: appreciative, laudatory, optimistic, admiring, enthusiastic.
  • Negative: critical, sarcastic, cynical, pessimistic, scornful, dismissive.
  • Neutral: objective, analytical, informative, descriptive, didactic (teaching).
  • Mixed: cautious, measured, sceptical, ambivalent.

Trap: academic passages are rarely "angry" or "ecstatic". Prefer moderate words like measured or cautiously optimistic.

6.Practice passage

Many companies moved their meetings online during the pandemic and never moved them back. Supporters point out that video calls save travel time and let teams in different cities work together. However, researchers have found that people on long video calls tire faster than in a room, partly because they watch their own face and must work harder to read small expressions. The answer is not to abandon video meetings but to use them with more discipline: shorter calls, cameras optional when they add nothing, and a clear agenda sent in advance.

Answer in your head, then tap to check.

Q1. The main idea is…

Video meetings are useful but tiring, so they should be used with discipline. (Not "video meetings should be abandoned" — the author rejects that.)

Q2. The author's tone is best described as…

Balanced / measured. Gives both sides, then a practical suggestion. Not critical, not enthusiastic.

Q3. Which word signals the turn to the author's own view?

"However" — the key point follows it.

Q4. True, false or can't say: "Video calls are more tiring than phone calls."

Can't say. The passage compares video calls with in-room meetings, not phone calls. Out of scope.

One line: Skim for the main idea, read for signal and tone words, and prove every answer with a line from the passage — cut extreme and out-of-scope options first.

Sheet 3 · error spotting

Spot the error

Grammar rules most tested · Cambridge Dictionary for usage checks

0.Scan order (every sentence)

Subject–verb → Tense → Article → Pronoun → Preposition → Parallel → Modifier → Comparison → Extra words

Read the whole sentence first. Find the real subject (ignore phrases in between). If nothing is wrong, mark "No error" — it is a valid answer.

1.Subject–verb agreement

✗One of the students are absent.✓One of the students is absent. ✗Each of the boys have a bag.✓Each / every / either / neither + singular → has. ✗The captain, along with his players, were late.✓was — "along with / as well as / with" don't add subjects. ✗Neither the teacher nor the students was ready.✓were — verb agrees with the nearer subject. ✗The number of cars are rising.✓The number … is · A number of … are.
!! never plural

information, advice, furniture, luggage, equipment, news, feedback, research → no "-s", singular verb. "Many informations" → much information.

2.Tense

✗I am living here since 2020.✓I have been living / have lived here since 2020. ✗If it will rain, we will stay in.✓If it rains — no "will" after if / when / unless / before. ✗The train left before we reached.✓had left — earlier past action = past perfect. ✗I am knowing the answer.✓I know — stative verbs (know, believe, own, prefer) avoid -ing. ✗Did he went?✓Did he go? — did / does + base form.

3.Articles go by SOUND

  • an hour, an honest man, an MBA, an FIR (vowel sound).
  • a university, a European, a one-way street, a useful tip (y / w sound).
  • the + unique things, superlatives, rivers, oceans, ranges: the sun, the best, the Ganga, the Himalayas.
  • No article: meals, games, languages, most proper names — "play the cricket", "learn the French".

4.Prepositions

WrongRight
discuss aboutdiscuss the plan
prefer tea than coffeeprefer tea to coffee
different than / todifferent from

senior / superior thansenior / superior to
comprises ofcomprises / consists of / is composed of

5.Pronouns

  • between you and me (I) — after a preposition use object form.
  • He is taller than I (am).
  • Who = subject (who called?) · whom = object (to whom did you write?).
  • Everyone must bring his or her / their ID — not "your".
  • Its = belongs to it · it's = it is.

6.Parallelism · modifiers · comparison

✗She likes reading, writing and to swim.✓reading, writing and swimming — same form in a list. ✗Walking down the road, the trees looked lovely.✓Walking down the road, I saw lovely trees — who was walking? ✗Delhi's climate is hotter than Shimla.✓than that of Shimla — compare like with like. ✗This is more better.✓better — no double comparatives. ✗He is elder than me.✓older than — "elder" never takes "than".

7.Redundancy & Indianisms

Cut the extra word

return back · revert back · repeat again · cousin brother · free gift · the reason is because → that · each and every

Indianisms

"do the needful" → please send the file · "prepone" → bring forward · "I have a doubt" → I have a question · "out of station" → out of town · "revert" (= reply) → reply

Confusables

affect (v) / effect (n) · principal / principle · lose / loose · then / than · advice (n) / advise (v) · stationary (still) / stationery (paper) · compliment / complement

Question tags

He is coming, isn't he? · I am right, aren't I? · Let's go, shall we? · Nobody came, did they?

8.Drill — find and fix

Cover the answer, find the error, then tap.

1. Neither of the two candidates were selected.

were → was (neither = singular).

2. Please find the attached informations below.

informations → information (uncountable). Better: "I have attached the information."

3. She has completed the course last year.

has completed → completed (finished time "last year" = simple past).

4. He is one of the best player in the team.

player → players (one of the + plural noun).

5. We discussed about the budget in detail.

Remove about.

6. I will call you when I will reach.

when I will reach → when I reach.

7. A European and a honest man met an university professor.

a honest → an honest; an university → a university. "a European" is correct.

8. The manager, as well as his team, are travelling today.

are → is (verb follows "the manager").

9. Kindly revert back at the earliest.

"revert back" is redundant and an Indianism → Please reply by Friday (give a date).

10. This phone is more cheaper than mine.

more cheaper → cheaper.

One line: Find the real subject, check verb and tense, then articles by sound, fixed prepositions, parallel lists, dangling modifiers and extra words — and remember "No error" is sometimes right.

Sheet 4 · subjective & written

Writing answers people can act on

Resources: SCQA · Power of 3 · Pyramid Principle · Tips for Strong Emails (British Council) · Business Writing (Purdue OWL)

1.Answering a subjective question

  1. Answer in the first line — don't warm up.
  2. Give 3 reasons (Power of 3), one short paragraph each.
  3. Add an example to each — real, specific.
  4. Close by restating the answer in new words.

Each paragraph = PEEL → Point · Evidence / example · Explain · Link back to the question.

2.SCQA — how to open anything

StepWhat it doesExample
SituationFacts everyone agrees onOur class submits group projects every month.
ComplicationWhat changed / the problemThree teams missed the last deadline.
QuestionWhat the reader now asksHow do we stop missed deadlines?
AnswerYour recommendationAdd a mid-point check-in one week before.

The Question is often only implied; S and C can be one sentence each.

3.Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto)

  • Start with the answer (the top of the pyramid), then the supporting points, then the data.
  • Group supporting points so they don't overlap and nothing is missing (MECE).
  • Each level answers the "Why?" or "How?" raised by the level above.
  • Busy readers can stop after the first line and still get the message.

Answer ▲ → 3 key arguments → facts / examples under each

4.Power of 3

  • People remember three items easily; three feels complete.
  • "Veni, vidi, vici" · "Stop, look, listen" · "Reduce, reuse, recycle".
  • Use it for: 3 reasons, 3 slides of body, 3 benefits, a 3-word closing line.

5.Email anatomy

PartRule
SubjectSpecific + action: "Project report draft — feedback by Fri 17 Oct"
GreetingFormal: Dear Dr Rao, · Neutral: Hello Priya,
Opening linePurpose first: "I am writing to request…"
BodyOne topic, short paragraphs, bullets for details
ActionWhat, by whom, by when
CloseBest regards / Kind regards + full name, role
!! email traps

Vague subject ("Hi", "Query") · "Dear Sir" when you know the name · ALL CAPS · no deadline · "PFA" / "do the needful" · forgot the attachment · no proofread.

6.Model email

Subject: Request to reschedule presentation — Group 4, 20 Oct

Dear Ms Kapoor,

I am writing on behalf of Group 4 to request that our presentation be moved from 20 October to 22 October.

Two of our five members are representing the institute at the inter-college hackathon on 20 October. The other groups have agreed to swap slots with us, so the schedule will not change for anyone else.

Could you please confirm the change by Thursday, 16 October? I have attached the revised schedule.

Kind regards,
Aarav Mehta
Group 4 Lead, Section B

Notice: purpose in line 1 · reason + solution · clear ask with date · attachment mentioned.

7.Business writing rules

  • Know your audience and your purpose before writing.
  • Active voice: "The team finished the report" not "The report was finished by the team".
  • You-attitude: write from the reader's side — "You will receive…" not "We have sent…".
  • Make it scannable: headings, bullets, bold the key date.
  • Positive framing: "You can collect it after 3 pm" not "You cannot collect it before 3 pm".

One line: Answer first (Pyramid), frame the problem with SCQA, support with three points, write emails with a specific subject, purpose in line one and a dated ask.

Sheet 5 · speaking

Speaking clearly and conversing well

Resources: 10 Ways to Have a Better Conversation (TED · Celeste Headlee) · Cambridge Dictionary · Forvo · BBC Learning English · Sounds of Speech (Univ. of Iowa)

1.Headlee's 10 rules (in short)

  1. Don't multitask — be fully present.
  2. Don't lecture; stay open to learning.
  3. Ask open-ended questions (who, what, why, how).
  4. Go with the flow — let thoughts come and go.
  5. If you don't know, say so.
  6. Don't equate your experience with theirs.
  7. Try not to repeat yourself.
  8. Stay out of the weeds — skip names, dates, details.
  9. Listen — with the intent to understand, not to reply.
  10. Be brief.

Master tip: be interested in other people, and be prepared to be amazed.

2.Structure a spoken answer — PREP

Point → Reason → Example → Point again

"I think group work builds real skills (P). It forces you to negotiate and divide tasks (R). In our web project, we had to split frontend and backend and agree on deadlines (E). So group work prepares us for actual jobs (P)."

"Tell me about yourself" → Present (who you are now) → Past (what brought you here) → Future (what you want next). 60–90 seconds.

3.Voice — the 5 Ps

PDo this
PaceSlower than feels natural when nervous
PitchVary it; a flat voice sounds bored
Pausereplace "um / like / basically" with a silent pause
PowerVolume for the back row
PronunciationClear consonants, correct word stress

Intonation: falling ↘ for statements and wh-questions · rising ↗ for yes/no questions.

4.Pronunciation essentials

Word stress

PHOtograph · phoTOgraphy · photoGRAphic

Noun ↔ verb: REcord / reCORD · PREsent / preSENT · OBject / obJECT

-ed endings

/t/ after voiceless: walked, laughed, kissed

/d/ after voiced: played, called, lived

/ɪd/ only after t or d: wanted, needed

Schwa /ə/

The most common English sound, in unstressed syllables: about, banana, problem. Weak vowels make speech sound natural.

Silent letters

debt · doubt · receipt · island · salmon · Wednesday · psychology · subtle

Often said wrong

vegetable (VEJ-tuh-bl) · comfortable (KUMF-tuh-bl) · determine (di-TUR-min) · hotel (ho-TEL) · suite (sweet)

Minimal pairs

ship / sheep · full / fool · vest / west · vine / wine — v (teeth on lip) ≠ w (round lips)

5.Which tool for what

  • Cambridge Dictionary — IPA + UK and US audio, example sentences, usage.
  • Forvo — words (and names) said by native speakers.
  • BBC Learning English — short lessons; pronunciation series for sounds and stress.
  • Sounds of Speech — animations of the mouth for each sound.
Daily 10-minute drill

Look up 5 words in Cambridge → repeat the audio 3× → record yourself → compare.

One line: Listen to understand, ask open questions, be brief; answer with PREP; pause instead of filler; get word stress, -ed endings and v/w right.

Sheet 6 · group presentation

Presenting as a team

Resources: Your Body Language May Shape Who You Are (TED · Amy Cuddy) · Body Language & Nonverbal Communication (helpguide) · Power of 3 · SCQA

1.Structure

PartContentTime*
HookQuestion, surprising number, short story~10%
Agenda"Three things today: …" (Power of 3)short
Body3 points, each with evidence / example; SCQA for the problem~70%
ConclusionRecap the 3 points + call to action / memorable line~10%
Q&AWhole team, prepared answersrest

* rough split; follow your exam's time limit.

"Tell them what you'll tell them → tell them → tell them what you told them."

2.Teamwork on stage

  • Assign roles: opener, content speakers, closer, slide controller, timekeeper.
  • Hand-offs by name: "Now Riya will show how we tested it." Never "next slide… uh, who's next?"
  • Everyone knows the whole deck — anyone can cover for anyone.
  • Rehearse together with a timer at least twice.
  • When not speaking: face the speaker or the audience, stay still, look engaged.
  • Share Q&A topics in advance so two people don't answer at once.

3.Slides

  • One idea per slide; the title states the takeaway.
  • Few words, large text (≈24 pt+), a picture or chart where possible.
  • Same fonts and colours throughout; high contrast.
  • Never read the slides aloud — the slide supports you, you aren't its narrator.

4.Nonverbal channels

ChannelDo this on stage
Eye contactHold 3–5 s per person; cover left, centre, right
PostureFeet shoulder-width, upright, open chest
GesturesOpen palms, between waist and shoulders
FaceSmile at start; match expression to message
SpaceStep toward the audience for key points
Voice (paralanguage)Tone, pace, pauses — see Sheet 5
AppearanceNeat, coordinated with the team

Avoid: crossed arms, hands in pockets, swaying, looking at the floor, fiddling with a pen, back to the audience.

When words and body language disagree, people tend to believe the body. Keep them aligned.

5.Amy Cuddy — power poses

  • Expansive, open postures ("high-power poses") held ~2 minutes before a stressful evaluation (interview, talk) can make you feel more confident.
  • Body language shapes how others see us, and may shape how we see ourselves.
  • Her message: "fake it till you become it".
!! know the debate

Later studies supported the feeling of power, but did not replicate the claimed hormone changes. Say "may help you feel confident", not "changes your hormones".

6.The 7–38–55 myth

Mehrabian: words 7%, tone 38%, body 55% — only when someone talks about feelings or attitudes and their words and tone/face contradict.

It does NOT mean "93% of all communication is nonverbal".

7.Handling Q&A

  1. Listen to the full question; don't interrupt.
  2. Repeat or paraphrase it so everyone hears it and you get thinking time.
  3. Answer briefly — point + one reason or example.
  4. Don't know? "Good point — we didn't look at that; we'll follow up." Never bluff.
  5. Bridge back to your key message.

8.Beat the nerves

  • Memorise your first 30 seconds word for word.
  • Breathe slowly: in 4 counts, out 4 counts, three times.
  • Find 2–3 friendly faces in different parts of the room.
  • A short pause feels long to you and normal to the audience.

One line: Hook, three points, strong close; hand off by name; slides support, you speak; open posture and steady eye contact; repeat the question, answer briefly, never bluff.
